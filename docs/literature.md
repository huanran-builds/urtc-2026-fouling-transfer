Related Work

## Li et al. (2026)

**Li, J., Lu, X., Arun, P., Xu, J., Gallucci, F., Li, S., & Bogaerts, A.** “Machine learning to predict plasma-based CO2 conversion in dielectric barrier discharge reactors.” *Green Chemistry* **28**(23), 9804–9821 (2026). DOI: **10.1039/d6gc01077f**.

Li et al. assembled a literature-derived chemistry dataset and showed that conventional random train/test splitting substantially overestimated model performance relative to source-publication-grouped validation, demonstrating that publication-level structure can produce misleadingly optimistic results. They did not attempt retrospective batch correction or domain adaptation; instead, their recommendation was prospective—more standardized data collection and reporting going forward—leaving open how to recover generalization from a heterogeneous literature dataset that already exists.

## Xu et al. (2026)

**Xu et al.** “A natural language processing to causality framework for robust knowledge extraction of CO2 hydrogenation with batch effect control.” *npj Computational Materials* **12**, 191 (2026). DOI: **10.1038/s41524-026-02065-2**.

Xu et al. constructed a literature-extracted catalysis dataset and explicitly treated source DOI as a batch, using DOI-level batch-effect control and benchmarking methods including ComBat and RUV-PC to reduce between-study variation. Their predictive stage still used an 80/20 random split, while DOI grouping and batch control were applied primarily in the causal-analysis framework, so they did not test whether adaptation could improve prediction on a completely unseen source publication.

## Austin et al. (2025) — DEBIAS-M

**Austin et al.** “Processing-bias correction with DEBIAS-M improves cross-study generalization of microbiome-based prediction models.” *Nature Microbiology* **10**(4), 897–911 (2025). DOI: **10.1038/s41564-025-01954-4**.

Austin et al. introduced DEBIAS-M to improve cross-study generalization in microbiome prediction and explicitly evaluated an online setting in which a fitted model adapts to samples from a previously unobserved study by optimizing cross-batch similarity without using the held-out outcome labels. This closely matches the unlabeled target-study adaptation setting of our work, but their inputs are pooled microbiome datasets generated by separate studies rather than a literature-extracted table assembled from experimental results reported across publications.

## Sun, Feng, and Saenko (2016) — CORAL

**Sun, B., Feng, J., & Saenko, K.** “Return of Frustratingly Easy Domain Adaptation.” *Proceedings of the AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence* **30**(1), 2058–2065 (2016). DOI: **10.1609/aaai.v30i1.10306**.

Sun, Feng, and Saenko introduced CORrelation ALignment (CORAL), a general-purpose unsupervised domain-adaptation method that reduces domain shift by aligning the second-order statistics of labeled source features and unlabeled target features without requiring target labels. CORAL was developed and evaluated on computer-vision domain shifts rather than literature-curated scientific data, so it serves as a methodological tool for our adaptation step rather than a competing result on study-level literature batch effects.

## Synthesis

Taken together, the problem of publication-level generalization is documented by Li et al., batch correction on literature-extracted data is established by Xu et al., and unlabeled target-study adaptation is demonstrated by Austin et al. CORAL provides a general-purpose mechanism for performing such unlabeled feature-distribution alignment. What remains untested in this literature is whether unlabeled adaptation to a completely unseen source publication can recover predictive accuracy on a literature-extracted dataset, which is the question we address.
