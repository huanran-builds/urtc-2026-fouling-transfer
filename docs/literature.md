Related Work

## Li et al.: publication-grouped validation of literature-derived data

Jiayin Li, Xinpei Lu, Pranav Arun, Jing Xu, Fausto Gallucci, Sirui Li, and Annemie Bogaerts. “Machine learning to predict plasma-based CO₂ conversion in dielectric barrier discharge reactors.” *Green Chemistry* **28**(23), 9804–9821 (2026). [https://doi.org/10.1039/D6GC01077F](https://doi.org/10.1039/D6GC01077F).

Li et al. assembled 358 literature observations reported between 2010 and 2025 and evaluated a hybrid physics-informed neural network, random forest, and XGBoost model using five-fold cross-validation grouped by source publication, preventing observations from the same paper from appearing in both training and validation folds. In Section 4.2, both XGBoost and the hybrid model achieved average R² values above 0.91 under randomized five-fold cross-validation, compared with R² = 0.441 under publication-grouped cross-validation; the authors describe this disparity as “severe inflation introduced by data leakage when interdependent points from the same published study are permitted to span both training and validation sets.” They did not apply batch correction or unlabeled domain adaptation to remedy publication-level shifts; their proposed remedy was prospective—community-wide FAIR data practices and more standardized reporting and collection—leaving retrospective correction of an already-curated heterogeneous dataset open.

## Xu et al.: batch-effect control in literature-extracted catalysis data

WeiHang Xu, LiHua Bai, Ji Qi, XiaoYing Sun, BaiRan Wang, Zhen Zhao, and Bo Li. “A natural language processing to causality framework for robust knowledge extraction of CO₂ hydrogenation with batch effect control.” *npj Computational Materials* **12**, Article 191 (2026). [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-026-02065-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-026-02065-2).

Xu et al. curated 1,839 catalyst observations from 160 publications, treated source DOI as the batch identifier, and controlled between-study variation using within-DOI projection and DOI fixed effects with GMM cluster interactions, while benchmarking RUV-PC and ComBat variants. Their correction was designed for causal and knowledge extraction, however, and the predictive stage used an 80/20 row-level split; they did not adapt a trained predictor to an entirely unseen publication using only that publication’s unlabeled features and test whether prediction improved.

## Austin et al.: unlabeled adaptation to a held-out study with DEBIAS-M

George I. Austin, Aya Brown Kav, Shahd ElNaggar, Heekuk Park, Jana Biermann, Anne-Catrin Uhlemann, Itsik Pe’er, and Tal Korem. “Processing-bias correction with DEBIAS-M improves cross-study generalization of microbiome-based prediction models.” *Nature Microbiology* **10**(4), 897–911 (2025). [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-025-01954-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-025-01954-4).

Austin et al. introduced DEBIAS-M, which learns taxon- and batch-specific multiplicative correction factors and improves leave-one-study-out microbiome prediction without using outcome labels from the held-out study. In their Online DEBIAS-M setting, the training-study correction factors and predictive weights are frozen before the method adjusts only the new study’s correction factors through cross-batch similarity; the published paper explicitly describes adaptation to samples from “a previously unobserved study.” This establishes unlabeled target-study adaptation, but the inputs are pooled sequencing datasets generated by separate studies rather than experimental rows extracted from publications into a literature-curated feature table.

## Sun et al.: CORAL as a general-purpose adaptation method

Baochen Sun, Jiashi Feng, and Kate Saenko. “Return of Frustratingly Easy Domain Adaptation.” *Proceedings of the Thirtieth AAAI Conference on Artificial Intelligence* **30**(1), 2058–2065 (2016). [https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v30i1.10306](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v30i1.10306).

Sun et al. introduced CORrelation ALignment (CORAL), an unsupervised domain-adaptation method that transforms source features to align their second-order statistics with those of an unlabeled target domain. CORAL is a general-purpose method originally demonstrated on computer-vision benchmarks, not a competing result on literature-extracted scientific data; its use here tests whether a simple covariance-alignment tool can mitigate source-publication shift.

## Synthesis and remaining gap

Taken together, the problem of cross-publication generalization is documented by Li et al., batch-effect control on literature-extracted data is established by Xu et al., and unlabeled target-study adaptation is demonstrated by Austin et al.; CORAL supplies a general-purpose method for attempting that adaptation. What remains untested is whether unlabeled adaptation to an unseen source publication can recover predictive accuracy on a literature-extracted dataset, which is the question we address.

## Closest near-misses from the final novelty search

The final search found four DOI-resolvable near-misses. None combines a literature-extracted feature table, complete publication-level holdout, adaptation using only the held-out publication’s unlabeled covariates, and a demonstrated improvement in prediction on that publication.

- Jianmin Guo, Zhongling Wang, Shouyao Yang, Yufeng Xu, Ting Huang, Yong Yang, and Huiyong Hu. “Multi-task deep learning with transfer learning for UHPC compressive and flexural strength prediction and inverse mix design.” *Case Studies in Construction Materials* **24**, e06088 (2026). [https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cscm.2026.e06088](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cscm.2026.e06088). This is the closest literature-data precedent: it applies CORAL and staged transfer between a source dataset of 170 UHPC mixtures compiled from publications and a target dataset of 25 mixtures produced in the authors’ laboratory. The target is newly generated local experimental data rather than a completely unseen source publication, and the accessible article metadata does not establish a strictly target-label-free adaptation protocol.

- WeiHang Xu, LiHua Bai, Ji Qi, XiaoYing Sun, BaiRan Wang, Zhen Zhao, and Bo Li. “A natural language processing to causality framework for robust knowledge extraction of CO₂ hydrogenation with batch effect control.” *npj Computational Materials* **12**, Article 191 (2026). [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-026-02065-2](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41524-026-02065-2). Xu et al. control DOI-defined batch effects in a literature-extracted dataset, but their predictive evaluation uses a random 80/20 split and does not adapt to a wholly held-out publication.

- George I. Austin, Aya Brown Kav, Shahd ElNaggar, Heekuk Park, Jana Biermann, Anne-Catrin Uhlemann, Itsik Pe’er, and Tal Korem. “Processing-bias correction with DEBIAS-M improves cross-study generalization of microbiome-based prediction models.” *Nature Microbiology* **10**(4), 897–911 (2025). [https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-025-01954-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41564-025-01954-4). Online DEBIAS-M provides the strongest methodological precedent for label-free adaptation to a previously unobserved study, but it operates on pooled primary microbiome profiles rather than rows extracted from publications.

- J. Greenhall, D. Pereira, M. Prisbrey, P. Vakhlamov, C. Chavez, C. Pantea, and E. S. Davis. “Generalizing synthetic data-trained acoustic predictive models to real-world measurements.” *Frontiers in Acoustics* **4**, 1867954 (2026). [https://doi.org/10.3389/facou.2026.1867954](https://doi.org/10.3389/facou.2026.1867954). Greenhall et al. apply CORAL using labeled simulations and unlabeled real spectra, with real-world labels withheld during training, but this is synthetic-to-real adaptation rather than adaptation to an unseen publication in a literature-extracted dataset.
