# Significance

Antimicrobial resistance causes substantial mortality, while the antibacterial pipeline remains too small and insufficiently innovative; nanoparticle antibacterials are therefore being investigated as complementary candidates, not established clinical replacements.[1-3] Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is a standardized in vitro susceptibility measurement, whereas minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) measures killing under defined laboratory conditions and is not routinely used for every infection.[4,5] Predicting these endpoints could prioritize new nanoparticle experiments only if performance transfers beyond the publications used for training. Random row splits cannot establish that capability when multiple observations share a source paper, because study-specific protocols and reporting practices can appear in both training and test data. Publication-grouped validation tests the intended use case: prediction for a nanoparticle study whose labels are unavailable.[6,7] Our MIC and MBC results show that apparent random-split performance collapses on held-out publications, and species-level errors worsen as representation falls. Without cross-study generalization, the model cannot guide nanoparticle design.
